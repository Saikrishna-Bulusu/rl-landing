Trial and error alone lost to the rule

PPO practised on the GPU for 410,000 flights per world. Rolling its dice it reached 91.8%, below the rule. Forced to take its top choice every time, it swung between 10% and 92%.

The copy test

The same network, seeing the same inputs, taught by copying the planner instead: 94.1%, above the rule by 0.95 points (±0.49).

A better policy existed; PPO never found it. Yet the copy caught only 48% of the moments the planner overrides the rule. From raw positions, the network cannot redo a 20-second look-ahead.

Corridor world shown; random: 91.8% dice, 69.6% top choice. Copy test paired on the same 4,096 flights.

The first training run failed, and the interesting part is why. PPO is the trial-and-error method: it flies, gets a score at the end, and slowly adjusts. After 410,000 practice flights it still sat below the simple rule. Its "top choice" version, which is what a real drone would fly, swung wildly during training, down to 10% at one point. To find out whether the problem was the learning or the network, I ran a copy test: the same network with the same inputs, taught by being shown the planner's correct choice at every decision instead of by trial and error. That copy reached 94.1%, better than the rule. So the network was capable of a good policy and trial and error simply never found it: like a learner driver told only "fail" at the end of a 3-hour drive, never which junction went wrong. But the copy still fell short of the planner, because it only reproduced 48% of the planner's key calls: the planner simulates each manoeuvre 20 seconds ahead, and the network cannot do that in its head from raw positions.
