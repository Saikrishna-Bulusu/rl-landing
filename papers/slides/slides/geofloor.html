What the matcher needs to see

GeoAnchor matches the downward camera to a 2021 map; the floor is the 2023 photo, never the map's own. Flat-coloured house blocks hid 40% of every suburban picture; with the photo draped over roofs and trees it finds 3.5× more fixes. A heading 10° off halves them and brings wild ones.

Part five puts GeoAnchor into the Logan simulation for real. GeoAnchor finds where the drone is by matching what its downward camera sees against a map, here the 2021 aerial photo. For the test to be fair, Gazebo's floor is a different photo, the 2023 one, never the map itself. The picture on the right is the same spot from 75 metres, twice. Top: the houses as the flat pink blocks from the survey and trees as green balls; they hide about 40 percent of every picture of the suburb, and GeoAnchor found a fix in only 11 percent of frames. Bottom: the same houses with the photo draped over their roofs and the trees, which is what a real camera would see; 38 percent of frames get a fix, with a median error of 2 metres. On plain ground with no photo, it finds nothing at all. The right-hand chart is why the heading matters: GeoAnchor turns each picture by the drone's compass heading before matching, and if that is 10 degrees off it finds half as many fixes and some are wildly wrong.
