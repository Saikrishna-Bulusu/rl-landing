Trees, on real firmware, twenty different forests

The world was flat grey until now, so the claim that actually motivates a bigger marker had never been tested. Ten to thirteen trees, 15–25 m, trunk under a wider canopy, respawned fresh for every trial and paired: both arms fly the same twenty layouts, only the coarse marker differs.

Delivery p = 7.7×10−7, Fisher exact.

A 1.20 m marker forces the aircraft down into the canopy layer before it can decode anything, so the trees both hide the pad and stand in the descent path. At 1.80 m it acquires at cruise and never enters that layer.

Two failures, two causes

Crashing is geometry. Above the pad sits a column of open sky. Every crash happened below 4.2 m of it — 4 of 7 tight-column trials, 0 of 13 wide ones, p = 0.007.

Delivering is sight. Column width does not predict delivery at all (p = 0.48). Whether the marker was ever decoded does: of 11 that saw it, 5 delivered; of 9 that never did, none.

A fixed forest said crashes never happen. Five trials on one layout gave zero crashes in both arms, and the conclusion drawn was that the firmware failure mode is the abort, not the collision. Twenty varied layouts found four crashes. That one forest simply had no tight gap in it — a layout can be measured precisely and still be silent about an entire failure mode.
