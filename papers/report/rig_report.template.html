A 360-degree camera rig, and a magpie that fights back

You said the bird results didn't count, because the drone was handed the magpie's exact position and speed. And a magpie that dives in a straight line gives its whole path away at the first sighting. So overnight I built a six-camera rig, trained a detector on its own pictures, tracked the bird from those detections, made the magpie steer and attack from behind, and tested it all. First on the bench, then in the fast simulator, then live in Gazebo with PX4, with no true bird position anywhere in the loop.

1 · The rig

Six 1280×960 cameras with 110° lenses, 20 frames a second, like the navigation cameras on a Skydio. Four look out in a ring and one looks up, all on a 30 cm mast above the propellers. One looks down from a short boom below the landing legs. The first version sat just above the propellers: spinning blades in the pictures looked like birds, so it moved up the mast.

2 · Seeing the bird: detector and tracker

A small neural network (about 90,000 weights, nothing downloaded) was trained on %%N_PICS%% rig pictures that Gazebo labelled itself: each picture rendered with and without the magpie, and the difference is the bird. That includes %%N_NEG%% empty views over the landing pads. It's scored only on drone positions it never saw, with the pictures degraded the way a real camera would: sensor noise, blur, JPEG compression, brightness changes.

The tracker turns detections into "where it is and where it's heading". It gates each detection on direction, not position: with distance this uncertain, a position gate happily paired unrelated false alarms into a phantom bird. It reports a bird only after two sightings, and only if it's moving.

3 · A magpie that fights back

The new "hunter" magpie waits behind and above the drone, launches only from its rear sector, and keeps re-aiming at where the drone will be (turning at up to 2 g). It stops correcting 0.3 s before it arrives, which is its reaction time. It keeps the old bird's calibration: a drone that doesn't dodge is touched on about 12% of dives.

Dodging 2 s early against the old straight-line magpie: touched on 0.6% of dives, which is why the old results were too easy. Against the hunter: 12.2%. It simply re-aims. Checked in both the fast simulator and Gazebo (12.6% at Gazebo's 30 steps a second).

4 · What knowing the bird is worth

  • Handed the truth: the rule is best (64.0% clean on magpie flights). Headroom over the rule −0.2 points: nothing for learning to add. That's your objection, confirmed.
  • With the rig: headroom +2.5 ± 1.5 points (better decisions +0.9, knowing the future +1.6). Modest, but real.
  • Own two cameras: the most headroom (+3.9), because a decider can compensate for the blind rear.

Open problem: phantom birds.

Close up, the live rig does well: a diving magpie within 30 m is reported on 78–98% of frames, pointing about 4–8° from the truth (PX4's own heading estimate is 6° off). But while the real magpie is far away, clutter still produces a tracked phantom 35–60% of the time: landing-pad markers, tree shadows, roof edges. Fixes that helped: the mast (propellers out of view), a propeller-tip mask, 1,200 hard negatives, ignoring surveyed pads, and reporting moving birds only. Not solved yet. The fast simulator's rig uses scattered false alarms and does not reproduce these persistent phantoms, so it's optimistic at long range. Within 20 m it matches Gazebo closely.

6 · The learner

Why the learner can beat even the "all-knowing" planner's headroom (+2.5 points): that planner projects the bird along a straight line from its true position and speed. It knows the present, not how the hunter will react to the drone. Against a bird that keeps re-aiming, that's no upper bound.

Its gain is completing more deliveries around the bird, not dodging better. What exactly it learned needs a closer look, flight by flight. Your critique here would help.

7 · Caught and fixed tonight

  • Laggy videos: the recorder sampled a stalling loop. Now one frame per camera image, 20 per second (checked in every flight).
  • Labels included the magpie's shadow; the magpie now casts none.
  • The bench rendered on the laptop's Intel chip (0.12× speed); it's now forced onto the NVIDIA card.
  • PX4 skips its speed setting when attaching to a pre-placed drone, so the first two rig flights ended in PX4's safety fallback. The speed is now set by hand, and failsafes are counted per flight.
  • The magpie was placed by the lagging video thread, so the cameras photographed a stale bird. The flight loop places it now.
  • A position gate plus distance-from-size turned pairs of false alarms into phantoms. The gate is now on direction.
  • The propellers and legs were in view. Fixed by the mast and boom.
  • Landing-pad markers were read as magpies. Fixed with hard negatives and ignoring surveyed pads.
  • My own mistakes: an edit that broke urban_env's imports for two minutes, found before any job loaded it; and a stray pkill that stopped the comparison once.
